# National Caregivers Day: Caregiving Is Also Coordination

Friday, February 19, 2027 is National Caregivers Day, the third Friday in February. It's a day to recognize professional caregivers and the family members, friends, and neighbors who step in.

By Mike Whobrey

The Providers Association for Home Health & Hospice Agencies (PAHHHA) founded the day in 2015, and the first observance was in February 2016 ([Days of the Year](https://www.daysoftheyear.com/days/national-caregivers-day/), [CaregiverDoc](https://caregiverdoc.com/national-caregivers-day/)). It isn't a federal holiday. It's a yearly reminder that care work is easy to overlook, and that the people doing it deserve thanks.

A thank-you matters. So does looking at the system around the caregiver, because a lot of the work is keeping the day from falling apart.

## The part of caregiving that lives in someone's head

In 2025, 63 million American adults provided ongoing care to an adult or a child with a medical condition or disability. That's almost one-quarter of all U.S. adults, a 45 percent increase since 2015 ([AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving, Caregiving in the U.S. 2025](https://www.caregiving.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Final-2025-CGUS-Report.pdf)). Of those caregivers, 59 million were caring for an adult. Caregivers spend an average of 27 hours a week on care, and 24 percent provide 40 hours or more.

Coordination is part of the job for most of them. In the same report, 70 percent monitor the care recipient's health, 64 percent communicate with providers, and 58 percent advocate on their behalf. Only 58 percent say coordinating care with health professionals is easy, down from 65 percent in 2015. About one-third find care coordination challenging.

## What coordination looks like in a household

A caregiver often holds a lot of moving pieces at once: which medications are taken together, which are as-needed, and which are paused; when the pill organizer needs filling, and whether a refill is on the way; appointments that can't collide with the rest of the week; who can see a health record, and who can change it; and what to leave behind when someone else covers for an afternoon.

If that information lives only in one person's head, or in sticky notes and text threads, the next person is starting from scratch. This is a good day to thank the person holding all of that, and to put the system in a place more than one person can use.

## A practical way to spend the day

A few concrete steps help more than a generic post:

1. **Say thank you, specifically.** Mention a thing the caregiver actually does, such as keeping the medication list current.
2. **Offer a real break, with a real handoff.** If you can cover an afternoon, ask what you need to know, and write it down.
3. **Look at where the information lives.** If the medication list, the next appointment, and the "who to call" list are scattered, pick one shared place and date it.
4. **Include the caregiver in the emergency plan.** The person who does the day-to-day care shouldn't be the only one who can answer a responder's questions.
