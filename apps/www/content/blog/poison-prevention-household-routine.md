# Poison Prevention Week: Turn Safe Storage Into a Household Routine

National Poison Prevention Week is March 21 to 27, 2027. Most poison advice comes down to habits at home, so this post turns it into a routine you can run: a cabinet check, a few chores, a shopping list, and a plan for leftover medicine.

By Mike Whobrey

Congress created the week in 1961, and HRSA says it's held every third week of March ([HRSA](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/what-you-can-do/poison-prevention-week)). This year's dates are March 21 to 27 ([AwarenessDays.net](https://www.awarenessdays.net/national-poison-prevention-week/)). America's Poison Centers says more than 90% of exposures reported to local poison centers happen at home ([America's Poison Centers](https://poisoncenters.org/prevention/in-the-home)). That's why prevention starts at home.

Domi Ops is a household organizer, not a safety device. It can't tell you what's in your cabinet, but it can hold the routine so the habit sticks. Talk with your doctor or pharmacist about your own medicines.

## Do the cabinet check once

America's Poison Centers says to store these items up, away, and out of sight, in their original containers, or in cabinets secured with child-resistant locks: all medicines (including over-the-counter ones, vitamins, and supplements), tobacco and e-cigarette products, alcohol, laundry and cleaning supplies, pesticides, button batteries, hand sanitizer and other personal care products, and other chemicals. It also says there's no such thing as a 100 percent child-proof lock or container ([America's Poison Centers](https://poisoncenters.org/prevention/in-the-home)).

HRSA adds that cleaners shouldn't go in food containers like cups or bottles, and that mixing household or chemical products can create a dangerous gas ([HRSA](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/what-you-can-do/prevention-tips)).

Walk through each room once. Then write down what you found and where it lives. In Domi Ops, [notes](https://domi-ops.com/) are markdown and can be pinned and tagged, so a pinned "Safe storage" note gives you one place to look.

## Make it a chore

A one-time check helps. A repeated one keeps working. Domi Ops has chores with streaks, so you can add small jobs like these:

- Put medicines and cleaners back in their spot after use.
- Check that the cabinet latch still works.
- Compare the bottles to your medicine list.
- Check the carbon monoxide detector.

HRSA says to have a working carbon monoxide detector, best placed near bedrooms and close to furnaces ([HRSA](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/what-you-can-do/prevention-tips)). Put the check on the shared calendar, next to everything else the house is doing.

## Shop with the routine in mind

Shopping lists in Domi Ops group items by aisle, and recurring items refill themselves. A few things worth adding:

- A lock or latch, if a cabinet doesn't have one.
- A sealable bag, and cat litter or coffee grounds, for the disposal steps below.

America's Poison Centers says to read and follow labels and directions, including the storage and disposal instructions ([America's Poison Centers](https://poisoncenters.org/prevention/in-the-home)). Keep products in their original containers, so the label stays with the product.

## Keep the medicine side current

The health side of Domi Ops tracks medications and appointments for each person, shared with the people you choose ([Domi Ops Health](https://domi-ops.com/health)). It estimates when each medication runs out, reminds you a week ahead (or the lead time you choose), and tracks a refill from requested to received. Stopping a medication in the app doesn't remove the bottle from your cabinet. If a medicine has been stopped, ask your doctor or pharmacist what to do with what's left, and add a task so it doesn't sit there.

## Plan for disposal

The FDA says the best way to dispose of most unused or expired medicines is a drug take-back location or a prepaid mail-back envelope. If neither is available, check the FDA's flush list, and don't flush any medicine unless it's on that list ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-disposal-medicines/disposal-unused-medicines-what-you-should-know)).

If it isn't on the flush list, the FDA says to mix the medicine with something unappealing, like dirt, cat litter, or used coffee grounds, without crushing pills. Seal the mixture in a plastic bag, throw it away, and scratch out the personal information on the empty prescription label ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/safe-disposal-medicines/disposal-unused-medicines-what-you-should-know)). Some products, like sprays and lozenges, have their own instructions, so check the label or ask your pharmacist.

## Save the number

America's Poison Centers says to put the Poison Help number, 1-800-222-1222, in your phone and display it at home and at work. You can also text POISON to 301-597-7137 to save it ([America's Poison Centers](https://poisoncenters.org/prevention/in-the-home)). It's free, confidential, and open 24/7 ([HRSA](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/faq/calling-poison-help)).

Add it to your pinned safe-storage note. HRSA says to call 911 if someone has trouble breathing, trouble waking up, seizures, or other life-threatening signs ([HRSA](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/sites/default/files/poisonhelp/resources/brochure_safe-medicine-tips.pdf)).
